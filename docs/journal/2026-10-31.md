## Plans for E1-E5

Item 6 moved to section E. Ten hunches are more than one run, so they were split: E1-E5 here, in
[docs/hypotheses/experiments/E1-E5.md](../hypotheses/experiments/E1-E5.md), and E6-E10 next.

The rule the D reviews taught was applied from the start: anything that holds by construction is asserted, not
tested. Learning hunches are mostly about mechanisms, so much is asserted:

- a comparator answers an omission and a decaying filter cannot (E2);
- a prediction-error learner blocks (E3);
- Oja's rule bounds the weights (E4);
- a bound memory is completed from a part (E5).

What is tested is whether each mechanism brings the Program closer to the worm, and only where a source gives the
worm's behaviour; otherwise the plan says the test waits. E4 is the exception: whether a co-activated pair captures
unrelated cues depends on the network's dynamics, not on a rule, so it is tested on the network alone.

The leads were looked up in Crossref, and two abstracts read in Europe PMC (corrected after review; see below):

- **[Rankin, Beck and Chiba 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0166-4328(90)90074-O).** Added as the worm's own case for
  E1. The worm shows short-term habituation, dishabituation, sensitisation and retention for at least 24 hours.
- **[Rankin et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nlm.2008.09.012).** The revised list of habituation's properties.
- **Rankin and Broster 1992.** The first draft said Europe PMC does not index it. That was wrong (see below).

Three leads are not found as works, so they stay unverified: Sokolov 1963, Tolman 1932 (books, with only reviews in
Crossref) and Rescorla and Wagner 1972 (a chapter).

*Correction, after the second agent's review of the first draft.* The draft said three abstracts were read; it was
two. It also said Europe PMC does not index Rankin and Broster 1992. It does (PMID 1590951): its DOI is registered as
`10.1037//0735-7044.106.2.239`, with a doubled slash, so a query by the single-slash form finds nothing. Its abstract
reports the worm's habituation as faster and deeper, and its recovery faster, at short interstimulus intervals. That
is the worm's own evidence against a decrement with one recovery time constant, and it is now cited in E1. The review
also found two worm sources for E5, both now cited with their abstracts read:

- [Rankin 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0735-7044.114.3.496): context conditioning of habituation, with extinction
  and latent inhibition;
- [Bettinger and McIntire 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1601-183x.2004.00080.x): state-dependent olfactory
  adaptation under ethanol.

Five design flaws were fixed:

- **E1** tested by construction and never against the worm. Its foil's equations are now fixed and their properties
  asserted, and the test is the distance to the worm's curves.
- **E3** claimed blocking through the wrong learner. Pavlovian protocols go through the co-activation learner, whose
  blocking is an open question.
- **E4's margin** could be crossed by chance alone. It is now an excess over a no-pair control, with 50 stored
  patterns fixed.
- **E4's protocol** could not see the used-together rule. A resting-high condition and a third clause test it.
- **E2's filter** can answer an omission at one interval with an OFF response. The test now uses two or more
  intervals.

The second look found four more flaws, all fixed:

- **Parameter fitting.** No plan said how the Programs' parameters are set, so a Program with more of them could win
  in-sample. Each plan now fits on one part of the worm's data and scores on a held-out part, as C13-C24 does: E1 and
  E2 across interstimulus intervals, E3 across conditions, E5 at Rankin 2000's two intervals.
- **E4's load.** Its 51 patterns in 200 units were above a Hebbian network's capacity. The load is now 11 patterns,
  with every pattern checked to be a fixed point first.
- **E4's test count.** It was four, not three.
- **E1's "most".** It was overstated. The foil's analytic outcomes are listed honestly: six properties, four of them
  shown, one of those only in part. Its single recovery time constant predicts slower recovery after short intervals, the opposite of Rankin
  and Broster's report.

The third look found one more: E4's capacity check was taken on a network trained on the pair, so the capture it
measures could have blocked its own test. The check is now taken on the no-pair network.

### C4 Again, After Review

Written before it runs. The second agent's review found two faults in the account above. The join-tick explanation is
contradicted by the journal of 2026-10-30, whose two lives joined on ticks 69 and 67 and ended in the same closing
state. And the old-pin life had 8 intents refused as stale (`judged … refused_stale`, intent ticks 373 to 378): its

withdrawn: the cause of the different closing state is not established.

The test, fixed before it runs: `life.sh` at the old pins, from the same build as above, up to five times. The first
life with no refused intent (`grep -c refused_stale life.log` is 0) is compared with the new-pin life. If its closing
state matches the new pin's to the printed digits, the refusals are the likely cause, and the bump changes nothing in
the placeholder's life. If it differs, the cause is still open and is investigated before the bump merges. If none of
the five is free of refusals, that is reported.

Two smaller omissions, recorded here:

- `arena.sh`'s own life step failed again on the library's name at both pins (`[FATAL] … no library at
  …/libplaceholder_worm.so`, `exit 1`), and `life.sh` stood in, as on earlier nights.

#### Result

## The Benchmark's Test, Built Red-First

This is item 2 (e1) of STATE.md: the classifier two-sample test that PLAN.md names
([Lopez-Paz and Oquab 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545)), in a new crate, `crates/benchmark`. It uses no
dependency beyond Rust's standard library. No real worm has been through it yet.

**What it does.** It takes two sets of worms, one row of measures each, and works in five steps:

1. It scales every measure over the pooled rows, without looking at which set a row came from.
2. It deals the rows into five folds by a seeded shuffle.
3. It classifies each row by a vote of its 21 nearest neighbours in the other folds.
4. It recomputes that accuracy under 999 seeded shuffles of the labels.
5. It reports the share of shuffles at least as accurate as the real labels, plus one, divided by 1,000, as the
   p-value. The sets count as told apart at p ≤ 0.05.

The same inputs and seed always give the same answer.

**Two departures, both stated in the crate's documentation.**

- **From PLAN.md.** PLAN.md speaks of a confidence interval around chance. The test uses a permutation null instead,
  for the reason given in § The Benchmark, Split Into Runs above.
- **From Lopez-Paz and Oquab.** They judge one held-out test set against a binomial null. Here every row is
  classified once by cross-validation, and those predictions share training rows. No unbiased estimate of the
  variance of a cross-validated accuracy exists
  ([Bengio and Grandvalet 2004](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v5/grandvalet04a.html)), so the permutation null, which
  needs none, takes the binomial's place.

**Choosing the number of neighbours, on synthetic samples only.** The first draft used 5 neighbours. Its unit test
asked that a one-standard-deviation shift in one measure of four, 60 worms a side, be told apart in every one of ten
pairs, and it failed at seed 7: accuracy 0.475, p = 0.675. So the test's power was measured rather than assumed, over
100 pairs a cell, 60 worms a side, with 199 shuffles (as in the unit tests). The command is
`cargo test --release -p benchmark power_table -- --ignored --nocapture`, run with `rustc 1.99.1`. The pairs come
from seeds 9000 to 9099, and the test itself is seeded 0 to 99.

| Neighbours | Same distribution | 1 sd shift, 1 of 4 | 1 sd shift, 1 of 8 | Spread ×1.6, 1 of 4 |
|------------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| 5 | 5 (2-11) | 60 (50-70) | 50 (40-60) | 21 (13-30) |
| 11 | 3 (1-9) | 78 (69-86) | 58 (48-68) | 18 (11-27) |
| 21 | 4 (1-10) | 84 (75-91) | 71 (61-80) | 17 (10-26) |
| 31 | 3 (1-9) | 88 (80-94) | 76 (66-84) | 11 (6-19) |

Each cell is a count out of 100 pairs, followed by its exact (Clopper-Pearson) 95% interval in per cent. The same command
prints the intervals.

What the table can and cannot separate:

- **The level is held.** At every setting the first column is consistent with 5 in 100, but only to within about
  five points either way.
- **k = 5 is weaker on a shift.** It is clearly weaker than every larger k there, with intervals that barely touch or do
  not overlap. That is the one difference the table settles.
- **Nothing else is separated.** Among 11, 21 and 31, the differences on a shift (78, 84, 88) and on spread (18, 17,
  11) lie inside each other's intervals.

The pairs are shared across k, so a paired comparison would be somewhat sharper, but none was run. So 21 is fixed for
what it is: a middle value among settings this table cannot tell apart, chosen because 5 is plainly worse on a shift.
It is not shown to be the best. These are synthetic samples, so no real worm decided it.

**The weakness this shows.** At 60 worms a side, the classifier catches a change in the spread of one measure only
about one time in six. The per-measure comparisons of (e2) must cover that: a comparison of whole distributions, not
only of centres, is the natural choice there. This is written into STATE.md's (e1) line so that (e2) inherits it.

**The tests.** Eight unit tests run in the suite. They cover:

- the level: no more than 3 of 20 identical pairs may be told apart;
- the power at a 1.5 sd shift: all 10 pairs must be told apart;
- determinism;
- a constant measure changing nothing;
- refusal of bad inputs (too few rows, ragged rows, NaN, an even number of neighbours, alpha of 1);
- that a row is judged only by rows in the other folds (added after review);
- the p-value's floor of one over one plus the shuffles (added after review);
- that 30 worms against 90, from one distribution, are told apart no more than 3 times in 20 (added after review).

Each was seen failing by breaking the code it guards, then restoring it:

| Break | Test that failed |
|-------|------------------|
| Always "told apart" | The level |
| Never "told apart" | The shift |
| The generator seeded from a global call counter | Determinism |
| The NaN check removed | Bad inputs |
| Dividing a constant measure by its zero spread | **Nothing, at first** |
| Leave-one-out in place of the other folds | Other folds |
| The p-value without its +1 | The floor |
| Chance taken as a fixed accuracy of 0.6 | Unequal samples |

The constant-measure test only asked for a finite accuracy, and NaN distances still give one. It now asks that adding
a constant measure leave the whole outcome unchanged, and with the division by zero put back it fails. A test that
had never failed had indeed not shown that it could.

## C18 Unparked: the Cliff Defined, the Test Piloted

C18, *spreading activation has a cliff*, was the lab's hardest parked plan: its first model refuted the hunch by
construction (journal of 2026-10-14, *Experiment Plans for C13-C17, and C18 Parked*), the pilot run to a steady state found
no steady narrowing with size (*C18's Model, Piloted*, the same night), and the width test that pilot led to cannot tell a
continuous transition from a smooth change (journal of 2026-10-15, *C18's Plan, Attempted Again*). The recorded next step
was to settle what the hunch's cliff means by reading its lead, which nobody had read, and then to plan a test of the
reached fraction below and above the threshold. Both are done; the plan is in
[C13-C24.md § C18](../hypotheses/experiments/C13-C24.md#c18-spreading-activation-has-a-cliff), and running it is item 11 of
STATE.md's *Next* list.

**The lead, read.** Shrager, Hogg and Huberman, "Observation of Phase Transitions in Spreading Activation Networks",
*Science* 236 (4805), 1092-1094, 29 May 1987
([doi:10.1126/science.236.4805.1092](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.236.4805.1092)). Crossref, OpenAlex and Europe PMC
carry the same three-sentence abstract, and PubMed lists it as PMID 17799664; OpenAlex, Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar
mark it closed, with no repository copy. A scan of the published pages is hosted publicly at
<https://gwern.net/doc/ai/nn/1987-shrager.pdf> (228 065 bytes, PDF 1.7, three pages, sha256
`cbb6d29485222538eb9fa553bda3ea4ac3306e78fd42c7cef7f27fe8284425ca`), whose title, authors, journal line, page numbers and
date match the publisher's record and whose margins carry the publisher's download watermark. It was read as text with
`pdftotext` and as page images, in a directory of its own, nothing executed. Confidence that it is the published paper as
printed: high. It is quoted here in short excerpts only. What it says:

- The model is linear: A(N) = C(N) + (1 - γ) A(N - 1) + α R A(N - 1), where A holds the units' activations, C the external
  input (one constant source in every experiment), γ in (0, 1) the relaxation rate, α the share of a unit's activity that
  flows to its neighbours per step, and R the link matrix, zero on the diagonal. **The budget is conserved**: "the
  activation from each node is divided among the attached nodes according to the weight of their connections", so "those
  columns of R that have any nonzero elements sum to one" - the hunch's second cure is the paper's own normalisation. The
  authors liken the procedure to current flow in an electrical network and the transitions to "percolation transitions seen
  in these electrical networks", here in graphs of any dimensionality. Nothing bounds the activation: the phase diagram
  assumes "no limits or bounds".
- Three parameters: μ, the average number of links per node; α; γ. The predicted phase diagram (Fig. 1, from Huberman and
  Hogg's theory, the paper's reference 8, then in press) has a horizontal boundary at α/γ = 1 and, above it, a vertical one
  at μ = 1. Below the first (region I) the net relaxes quickly to a fixed point and the activation stays near the source,
  within a finite "event horizon"; above it with μ < 1 (region II) that horizon grows without end in time but stays local in
  space; above it with μ > 1 (region III) it grows in space too, across "a sharp transition in which the number of nodes
  with activation values above a given positive threshold grows explosively".
- Four computational experiments, each 25 trials on random graphs of 100 nodes with one constant source, settled when the
  total activity changes by less than ε = 0.001 between iterations and stopped at a bound B on the total activity.
  Experiments 1 and 2 sweep the number of links from 20 to 100 (μ from 0.4 to 2.0) at α/γ = 1.5 with B = 10 and 1000: "As
  expected from the limited size of our network, the transition is smoothed out. To highlight the transition, we examined
  the relative fluctuations" - the standard deviation of the fraction of active nodes over the trials divided by its mean -
  which "peaks at approximately the predicted point of 50 links (μ = 1)" (Fig. 2). Experiments 3 and 4 raise α/γ from 2/3
  to 1.5 at 20 and at 70 links with B = 100: "One can clearly see the predicted sudden growth in activity when α/γ = 1.0.
  Below this point the network always settles. Above it, the activity continues to grow and eventually reaches B", and the
  time to settle or to reach B peaks at the transition (Fig. 3).
- On size: "The finite size of any real network, as well as the limited time over which experimental observations are
  made, introduce corrections to this theory that result in a smoothing of the transitions", and "near the transition
  points, relative fluctuations in behavior become very large". The conclusion: "instead of the fairly smooth transitions in
  performance that have been generally assumed in these situations, we have shown that abrupt transitions can be expected."
- One discrepancy inside the paper: Fig. 2's caption gives α = 0.4 and γ = 0.6 for experiments 1 and 2, the text gives
  α = 0.6 and γ = 0.4, "so α/γ = 1.5"; only the text's values put that path above the α/γ = 1 boundary, where Fig. 1 draws
  it. The text's values are taken.
- What it does not do: it runs one size, 100 nodes, and varies the bound B rather than N, so it measures nothing about

**What this settles.** Read through its lead, the hunch's cliff is a phase transition in the physicist's sense: a threshold
at which the reach of the spread turns from finite to extensive, sharp only in the limit of size. The lead has two. The
topological one, at mean degree one, is the random graph's percolation threshold - continuous, its order parameter rising
from zero, which is exactly the case the width test of the first two attempts could not see - and it exists only in the
growing regime, α/γ > 1. The dynamic one, at α/γ = 1, is where the linear system loses its fixed point; with a cap on
activation, as in the lab's model and in the hunch's "hubs ... climb to their bounds", the growth saturates instead. In
the hunch's pure network every edge carries a fixed weight, so the gain grows with connectivity - a mean of 0.4 d in the
lab's model - and adding connections carries the network across the dynamic threshold at d = 2.5, where the giant
out-component already holds about nine units in ten in the limit (S = 1 - exp(-2.5 S) gives S = 0.89); that is the hunch's
cliff, and the probe of 2026-10-15 saw it as a jump. The lead's conserved budget pins the gain at α/γ whatever the
connectivity, which is how the hunch's second cure can work; the lead's own result adds that above a gain of one the
topological cliff survives the budget. So the test proposed on 2026-10-15 is the right one for either kind of threshold:
below it the reached fraction falls about as 1/N, above it it holds, and a smooth change shows neither. Not settled:
whether a cure removes the threshold or only moves it, which is the plan's own question; and what Huberman and Hogg's
theory says in detail, which waits on the unread paper.
